> Research notes: key claims and short quotes, keeping the original's section numbers and headings. Not a copy of the original.

## The Resume at a glance

- The guide keeps two versions: a LaTeX/Overleaf PDF (better-looking) and a Google Doc (more ATS-friendly), because LaTeX PDFs "can be problematic" for some ATS (links an Academia Stack Exchange thread).
- Channel rule: where "there is no guarantee a real human will see it, like LinkedIn quick apply or Indeed job forms," it recommends the Google Doc version. When a recruiter or hiring manager will see it, the LaTeX PDF.
- Cites "99%" of Fortune 500 using ATS and "75% of resumes fail to pass ATS" (third-party marketing statistics, unreliable).

## General Principles

- No spelling or grammar mistakes. "Keep it 1 page, unless you have 10+ years of experience." Consistent font sizes, sparing bold and italics. No fancy formatting, graphics, images, or icons. Nothing that invites bias (age, gender, nationality). Bullet points, not blocks of text. Google Doc fonts: Times New Roman, Calibri, Georgia. "If you have time, tailor every section in your resume to the corresponding job description."

## Header

- Name, an optional job title ("how you see yourself"), phone and email, city and country, relevant links (LinkedIn, GitHub, Medium, Kaggle). Make sure the links work.

## Summary Statement

- Unnecessary "if your resume clearly states what you have done and who you are," and the guide's own resume doesn't use one. Useful for tailoring to a company or role, "or even play the ATS a bit."
- Don't use "passionate", "hard working", or "determined": "That's the baseline of anyone a company wants to hire."
- A good one states who you are and what you've done in a couple of sentences. His: a DS/MLE with 4+ years "specialising in time series forecasting, operations research / optimisation problems and applied machine learning," with domain expertise in insurance, supply chain, and logistics.

## Technical Skills

- A brief summary that "should not really exceed 4–5 lines." Placed high so recruiters can check technical fit.
- Don't list too many technologies: "Python, SQL, C++, Rust, Assembly" looks like buzzwords.
- Use "proficient" or "familiar with" for coding languages, never star ratings ("Python 4/5") or "advanced." His bar for proficient: comfortable with easy LeetCode problems in that language.
- Don't list every package. For data science it assumes NumPy, Pandas, and Matplotlib. List differentiators instead (Git, AWS, Argo, Bash, Databricks).

## Experience/Projects

- Show what you did at each company and the outcome, "always using numbers and figures. Ideally, they would be financial impact." Don't be humble.
- Name specific models (ARIMAX, XGBoost), the metric they improved, and the business impact. "Companies only care about the financial benefit you bring them ... Profit is profit."
- Four-step framework for every bullet: what you analyzed, predicted, or modeled; the technologies, algorithms, and statistical tools; the metrics improved; the business value generated.
- Be explicit about technologies, packages, and algorithms. It beats vague language and helps with ATS.
- Paid work only (research is fine), reverse chronological, internships distinguished from full-time roles, no sub-bullets.
- No experience: replace with a Projects section in the same technical-plus-business wording, most relevant projects first.

## Education

- Without relevant work experience: Education before Experience, then Projects.
- He keeps it simple with 4+ years of experience, but keeps it because many DS/ML roles require a STEM master's.
- Don't list all your modules ("no one really reads that"). Grade only if impressive. Hackathons, projects, awards are fine. Leave out course certificates unless they're recognized (e.g. AWS practitioner) or you have spare space.

## Activities / Extracurricular

- Optional, a little personality is fine, but "this would be the first section to cut if you are lacking space."
