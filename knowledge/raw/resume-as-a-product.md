## Framing

- "You should treat your resume like a product," a landing page with four target users. The same advice largely applies to LinkedIn.

## Part 1: Your resume is a product (four readers)

- **Machines (ATS)**: parse the resume into fields (name, contact, history, education, skills) and score against the JD on keywords, years, and recruiter-set criteria. Modern AI search knows "built ML pipelines" ≈ "machine learning infrastructure", so "you don't need to stuff your resume with every keyword," but accomplishments must be "substantively relevant." Needs: clean, parseable formatting (tables, some multi-column layouts, text boxes, icons, images break extraction; creative titles like "My Journey" are risky), minimum qualifications easy to find, JD keywords, technologies named where they can be indexed.
- **Recruiters**: an in/out call "in 5–7 seconds" after skimming the summary and a few bullets. Need: minimum requirements visible immediately (years, degree, similar titles), "a quick visual path to something that excites them" (brand-name company, a relevant project, the role's tech), brevity: "keep it to one page unless you're a CEO."
- **Interviewers**: screeners look for conversation starters. Onsite interviewers gauge level from business impact and watch for red flags (short stints, gaps). Need: accomplishments that fit the team's work, technical detail to defend claims, signals of depth and leadership, "a minimum of reasons to be suspicious."
- **Hiring managers**: the most thorough readers, of a few finalists. Need: past work resembling the job, "measurable impact with appropriate scope," ownership and growth signals, a clear visual hierarchy.

## Part 2: Content

- **The Basics**: expected order roughly Contact → Summary (2-3 sentences) → Work Experience → Education → Projects → Skills. Vary it by profile: experienced candidates de-emphasize education, new grads surface projects. "Readers expect to find things in roughly this order, so don't make them hunt."
- **Contact**: name, email, phone if you want calls, a linked LinkedIn URL. GitHub or a personal site only if impressive: "GitHub profiles need at least some recruiting-friendly repos that are deployed somewhere and well-documented." US citizens: note citizenship if it isn't obvious.
- **Write a Summary**: "always include a summary and put it at the top." Two or three sentences, tailored, because "recruiters will heavily depend on this summary." Warning: "Don't let AI write your summary." AI summaries "all sound the same." "'results-driven,' 'passionate,' and 'cross-functional teams' are filler. They fit any candidate, which means they describe none of them." His better example: "I make complex data feel simple. I'm a front-end engineer with 4 years experience building B2B decision platforms at multiple fast-moving startups."
- **Writing Bullets**: "Resume readers are hiring you to deliver business value using technology." Lead with results ("Delivered [business value] using [technology/approach]"). Quantify and show scope. "Your bullets become interview topics," so make them interesting and defensible. Include leadership signals. "Lead with your best," not chronological order. Embed technologies in accomplishments. Example pairs:
  - "Worked on the checkout system and fixed bugs" → reduced checkout abandonment 12% by fixing a payment-flow race condition (React, Stripe API).
  - "Responsible for migrating the database" → led a 6-month migration of 50M records MySQL → PostgreSQL across 3 teams with zero downtime.
  - "Helped improve the CI/CD pipeline" → cut deploys from 45 to 8 minutes, adopted by 12 teams.
  - "Mentored junior engineers" → mentored 3 through first launches, one promoted within 8 months.
- **Projects & Proof of Work**: valuable only for (1) new grads with little paid experience and (2) people moving into a new technical area ("*cough* AI"). Otherwise "projects are marginal." Prioritize paid work. GitHub only with polished, deployed, documented repos. Side projects show initiative, so skip generic descriptions ("AI-powered todo app in React Native"). Technical writing: make the 2-3 best pieces easy to find.
- **The Skills Section**: a ranked "skill soup" for keyword search. "Keep it limited to what you actually know," since interviewers probe long lists. (Listing "HTML" is mocked.)
- **Tailoring**: most people don't tailor every job. Tailor for big-name companies and keep a few role-type versions. Read the JD for (1) repeated terms, (2) requirements vs wish list ("Focus your tailoring energy on what appears at the top"), (3) the core hiring problem ("own our data pipeline" → reliability and ownership), (4) specific technologies vs general skills vs filler ("Strong communication skills" is filler). Trick: ask an LLM for the JD's top 10 concepts and compare your resume. Tactics: adjust the summary, reorder sections (lift AI projects above experience when applying to AI roles without paid AI work: "leaving those projects at the bottom is a sure way to get ignored"), reorder bullets, expand or condense roles, match skills.

## Part 3: Design

- Most important things at the top of the page and of each section. Clear top-to-bottom flow. Size and weight guide the eye, "be careful with color."
- **Formatting for Both Machines and Humans**: simple formatting. A PDF that "exports cleanly to text," testable by uploading to Google Docs. One page. Space savers: margins down to 1/2", company + title + years on one line, older roles compressed to one line ("the farther the experience is down your resume, the less likely a reader is to actually read it"), and a two-column layout with contact, skills, and education in a side column (note: contradicts its own Part 1 warning about multi-column layouts). Use bolding and hierarchy. "Don't overthink templates."
- **Making Your Experience Scannable**: make recognizable names visible, "readers only read the first part of each section." Surface minimum qualifications. Give recent and flagship experience the most space.
- **Handling sticky situations**: remove month indicators to hide intra-year gaps. If recently let go, it suggests not adding an end date for 6-12 months (dishonest, not adopted). A one-line explanation for startups that folded.

## Part 4: Common Mistakes

- Too many buzzwords ("Results-driven," "synergy," "leverage"): if a bullet could be swapped with another candidate's, it's too generic. Zero impact ("Worked on the payments team"). Hard-to-read templates ("a reader should be able to make a decision about your resume in about 3 seconds"). Irrelevant information. Walls of text.
- What not to include: age, photo (US), full address, irrelevant old jobs, social links other than LinkedIn, "references available upon request," hobbies unless relevant, ATS tricks ("packing your resume with white text"), which recruiters and ATS engineers "despise."

## Closing Thoughts

- "Your resume is never done." Iterate like a product: add what interviewers ask about, cut or prepare bullets you bombed. Get human feedback, ideally from someone who has hired. "It usually takes 3-4 revisions before it's good."
