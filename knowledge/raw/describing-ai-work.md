## §1 Thesis

- AI made polished resumes cheap, so polish no longer signals quality. "If you can't describe your AI work clearly, it doesn't exist."
- Real AI work gets missed because it's described poorly, while hype bullets fill the space.
- The test: could someone reading without context tell "what you built, how it worked, and why it mattered?"

## §2 Nine failure modes (with the guide's examples)

1. **Vague Observer**: interest without a built artifact ("Following trends in generative AI and LLMs"). Belongs "in a cover letter at best."
2. **Tool-Dumper**: tool names, no task or outcome ("Used GPT-4.1, Claude 3.5, Zapier, LangChain, Pinecone, and Notion to improve operations"). "Tool lists are not accomplishments."
3. **Inflated Generalist**: platform-scale language for a side project ("Architected end-to-end AI solution", "Spearheaded agent-based multi-modal infrastructure strategy"). Collapses under questioning.
4. **Undersold Real Work**: "the most tragic category". Real work described generically ("Worked on AI tooling for customer support"). Name what the AI did, the workflow, whether it was used and trusted.
5. **Generic Contribution**: "Collaborated on LLM integration." "'Collaborated' is a weasel word when not followed by specifics." Don't claim a teammate's AI work, but own your slice.
6. **Buzzword Blender**: jargon stacks ("multi-agent RAG pipelines leveraging zero-shot semantic clustering"). "If your bullet reads like it was generated by GPT trying to sound impressive, it's probably not helping you."
7. **Over-Owner**: implying you did all of it ("Built Claude-powered Slackbot to power all of customer success"). "There's a huge difference between 'built' and 'contributed to.'" Precision reads as trustworthy.
8. **Disconnected Win**: a good outcome with the AI's role unclear ("Reduced onboarding time by 30% through AI enhancements").
9. **Prompt-as-Product Illusion**: a one-off prompt framed as a release ("Launched agent for automated investment recommendations"). Talk about the iteration instead.

- Closer: a summary graphic of the five missing elements. "You don't need all five in every bullet. But if you're missing all of them, you're signaling nothing."

## §3 Using AI on your own resume

- **Wrong ways**: overgeneralizing ("Implemented cutting-edge AI innovations across business units"), overpolishing (removes "every ounce of personality"), overclaiming (invents "Saved $1.2M ..."; "you're lying").
- Treat the model as "a smart, fast, but slightly overeager junior editor."
- **Step 1, real substance first**: write what you did plainly, like an email to a colleague. Partial details are fine, talking it out helps recall.
- **Step 2, specific instructions**: ask for one bullet with the tool, the task, the measurable outcome, honest wording. Example result: a GPT-4 Slackbot triaging tickets into Jira, 85% match with human categorization, ~5 hours/week saved.
- **Step 3, alternate framings**: ask for a more technical and a more impact-focused version, or which tradeoff would show judgment.
- **Step 4, reflection**: feed in 5-10 bullets and ask what themes and recurring problems show up.
- Closer: "AI is a powerful mirror. But it will reflect back whatever tone you feed into it. Don't let it erase your personality."

## §4-§8 By function (each with weak bullets, rewrites, project ideas, a 5-point audit)

The same five audit questions repeat in every section, phrased for the role: name a real model or tool; say what the AI actually did; ground it in value; give a measurable or observable outcome; show judgment (tradeoffs, fallbacks, constraints, iteration). "Hit 3 out of 5, you're already stronger than most." 5 of 5 is roughly the top 1%.

- **§4 PMs**: weak "Explored generative AI use cases", "Collaborated with engineers to integrate LLM functionality". Strong bullets name the model, what it did, what the PM did, an outcome, and judgment (e.g. evaluating two models on support-ticket summaries and choosing one "for tone control + accuracy"). Projects: support synthesizer, feature prioritization bot, microcopy rewriter, model evaluation doc, AI risk memo ("PMs who understand restraint are rare").
- **§5 Engineers**: "In 2023, it was enough to say you'd built something with GPT. In 2024, the bar is higher." Hiring teams ask about evals, retries, fallbacks, caching, production monitoring, and "where not to use AI." Weak: "Built LLM-based chatbot using LangChain and Pinecone" (reads like a tutorial clone). Strong rewrites name the task, model, system behavior (retries, evals, fallbacks), and outcome, e.g. an HR chatbot answering 2,000+ questions with hybrid search and a fallback-to-human trigger added after two weeks of evals. Projects: hybrid RAG chatbot, eval framework, function-calling agent, prompt-chain debugger.
- **§6 Designers**: "how the system behaves is your material." Strong bullets name a model behavior (uncertainty, hallucination), the UI pattern that handles it, and a test result. Projects: trust patterns for hallucinations, correction feedback loops, compare-and-choose UI, uncertainty legends.
- **§7 Ops / BizOps / Chiefs of Staff**: AI work here is often invisible. Strong bullets name the tool, the task, the impact, and the system context (where it runs, who uses it). Projects: executive update generator, intake classifier, scorecard drafter, meeting-note synthesizer, SOP auditor.
- **§8 Generalists and career switchers**: titles like "Prompt engineer" and "Exploring AI tools" are red flags. Self-directed work becomes credible with structure and outcome. Projects: multi-model comparison memo, workflow rebuilder, prompt pattern notebook, a public tool, applied AI in your own domain ("depth, not just breadth"). "You're allowed to be self-taught. You just can't be vague."

## §9 AI detectors

- Companies run tools like GPTZero on resumes and cover letters, and flagged documents are often dropped silently. "The danger here isn't that you cheated. It's that you sound like you did."
- Detectors judge predictability: **perplexity** (how surprising the text is) and **burstiness** (variation in sentence length and structure). Polished, uniform, mid-length prose looks machine-written: "the more polished and safe your writing is, the more likely it is to be flagged."
- If flagged: find the lowest-scoring sections, break up repeated structures ("Built X using Y to achieve Z"). "GPT loves safe verbs: leveraged, utilized, developed, enhanced. Replace them with tactile ones: rebuilt, debugged, restructured, scrapped, retrained." Vary rhythm.
- Read pre-2022 human writing to retrain your ear.
- Practical steps: (1) never paste AI bullets in directly, rewrite them; (2) run the final draft through GPTZero for competitive roles; (3) vary sentence structures; (4) use verbs that show real thinking and struggle; (5) "Trust that a little imperfection builds credibility."
- Closing: "legibility is leverage," and the resume is "a record of how you think."
