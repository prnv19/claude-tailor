## §1 Seven golden rules (recruiters and coaches)

- **Tip #1, answer the recruiter's questions immediately**: years of role-relevant experience (and of management, for manager roles). A recruiter: "we want to see if you meet the job requirements in the first 10 seconds!"
- **Tip #2, a non-traditional structure is allowed**: the traditional layout is "perhaps the safest. But it's not obligatory." One recruiter prefers two bullet-point summaries at the top: "The top of the resume is the prime real estate. Put the shiny bits, your best achievements, up top."
- **Tip #3, no design features**: A resume coach: "pictures, columns, photos, etc. can prevent ATS ... from correctly scanning your resume." No photo, which also conflicts with employment and discrimination law in most countries.
- **Tip #4, state the locations you're open to**, including remote, hybrid, relocation (e.g. "San Francisco | Remote | Hybrid within 30 miles").
- **Tip #5, cut all waffle**: A recruiter: "Putting 'Experienced engineer passionate about making great products' doesn't tell me anything. It wastes both space and seconds of the recruiter's time." Another coach: describe the problem solved, not just the models deployed (e.g. an NLP document suite "to help users retrieve semantically relevant documents").
- **Tip #6, numbers tell a better story**: quantify everything. A senior ML engineer: include "cost savings, revenue generated, efficiency improvements, or time saved."
- **Tip #7, a Skills section carries keywords**: "You don't want to jam your resume full of keywords," but list the necessary skills, tools, and technologies. A skills or "technology snapshot" section lets recruiters check fit quickly.

## §2 Seven key skills for an ML engineer resume

- **§2.1 Leadership**: show complex problems solved and mentorship or leadership, even for IC roles. Use projects or university if work lacks examples.
- **§2.2 Communication**: cross-functional work, stakeholder alignment for manager roles.
- **§2.3 Data analysis**: using data for decisions, not only model training.
- **§2.4 Facilitation**: unblocking a project, avoiding a bottleneck, improving a process.
- **§2.5 System design**: make any design experience prominent.
- **§2.6 End-to-end ML** (the most important, per a senior ML engineer): "Highlight your expertise across the entire ML lifecycle ... Recruiters look for candidates who understand ML holistically." Another coach: "mention the relevant tech stack in detail," which shows depth "but also increases the chance of passing automated resume screens." Another coach: don't list only one ML library, learn a second and add it. Career changers: emphasize ML-adjacent work (e.g. pipelines that feed models).
- **§2.7 Generative AI**: many roles want LLM and deep learning in the stack. Without professional experience, build a project that shows it.

## §3 Six real resumes (observations only, images not captured)

- **§3.1 Google senior MLE**: quantified impact in every role, a separate awards and highlights section, action verbs.
- **§3.2 Apple senior staff MLE**: 3.5 pages, "could use a little more streamlining," though experience justifies most of it. Career highlights summarized at the top. Shows ML used to solve problems in non-ML roles.
- **§3.3 Senior MLE at a social media company**: leadership and cross-functional work in each role, quantified results ("15% reduction in user-reported spam incidents").
- **§3.4 Google SWE-ML**: concise, technologies summarized up front.
- **§3.5 Google/Waymo ML SWE**: detailed projects with tech stack and quantified impact, publications and academic or volunteer work included at some cost in length.
- **§3.6 Amazon senior SDE-ML**: example only.
- Takeaway: "there are many acceptable ways to write a resume," and none of the examples follow every rule.

## §5 Section by section

- **§5.1 Study the JD and company**: work out which profile the JD wants. Pick its most important responsibilities and find matching examples with numbers. "Take note of the language used in the job description so you can, where appropriate, match specific verbs and phrases." Research company values and reflect them (Meta example: a volunteering interest for "build social value"). Ideally one version per role, usually a few strategic edits.
- **§5.2 Design**: "Keep it clean and simple." A creative design can put recruiters off and break processing. No photo.
- **§5.3 Sections**: Personal information, Work experience, Education, Skills/Tools, Interests/Extracurricular. Recent graduates (a year or two of experience) start with Education, which Google recommends.
- **§5.4.1 Personal information**: DO a larger font for the name, name + email + phone + city, a LinkedIn (or GitHub) link. DON'T title the section, include a street address ("unnecessary and unsafe"), a photo, date of birth, or gender, or label fields ("email:", "tel:").
- **§5.4.2 Work experience**: "Instead of listing responsibilities, you need to talk about actions." Start each bullet with an action verb tied to the key skills ("Executed," "Unblocked," "Led," "Delivered"). Cites the formula "Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y] by doing [Z]" (its own example, "Decreased server response time by 30% by implementing machine learning implementation," is weak wording).
  - DO: reverse chronological. Present tense in the current role except completed achievements, past tense otherwise. Include programming languages and libraries. Describe actions and results. Quantify. Match the JD's language. Include keywords naturally. Show a balance of skills.
  - DON'T: be shy, list responsibilities, be vague, "Go so overboard with numbers that it looks like a math problem," "Include lots of buzzwords just for the sake of it."
- **§5.4.3 Education**: after Personal information for recent grads, otherwise after Experience. DO: highest degree first, degree + school + dates, relevant subjects for recent grads, GPA and test scores, awards with their competitiveness ("two awards for 1,000 students"), bootcamps or courses if ML experience is thin. DON'T: high school once graduated, a thesis unless a recent grad (then explained simply).
- **§5.4.4 Awards & Leadership**: Google's recommended label. Two or three strong items or omit the section. DO: "Put awards in context. E.g. '1st out of 22 applicants'." DON'T: school awards 10+ years after graduating, weak filler ("employee of the week").
- **§5.4.5 Skills & Interests**: list skills in sentences to save space. DON'T list generic interests ("watching Netflix") or basic skills ("Google Docs", "MS Word").
- **§5.5 Proofreading and feedback**: proofread after every tweak, top to bottom and then in reverse. Save as PDF unless the JD says otherwise and check it opens on Mac and PC. Get feedback, ideally from an experienced recruiter.

## §6 Checklist (paraphrased)

- Presents you as the profile the JD wants.
- "Is it just one page? If not, do you have the experience to merit 2 or more pages?" Consistent formatting. "Is there enough white space to breathe?"
- Actions not responsibilities, impact quantified, a range of skills, languages and libraries listed, generative AI included.
- Awards post-university if you graduated 10+ years ago. Distinctive interests.
- Proofread since the last edit, feedback received, saved as PDF.
