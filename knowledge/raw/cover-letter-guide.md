## Premise

- "Most recruiters don't read cover letters anymore," but when they matter, most fail by being "too generic or try too hard to sound 'impressive.'" A good letter is "a clear, honest conversation between you and the hiring manager."

## §1 Start with the job description

- Focus on the "What you'll do" section and highlight its top 3 tasks: "Those are their biggest pain points." Example: answer "manage email campaigns" with a real newsletter result (200+ members, engagement up 40%).
- Don't over-weight requirements: "If you meet 60-70% of the 'must-haves,' apply." People land roles missing 2-3 requirements when they frame experience well (a 2-year candidate hired for a "5 years" role because the letter showed she understood the job).

## §2 Match your skills to their needs

- Two columns: their needs, your matching experience. Connect dots, don't reinvent yourself.
- "Use their language": if the posting says "CRM", say "CRM", not "customer database". It's "speaking their dialect," not tricking anyone.
- When stuck, ask "What's the core problem this job solves?" and show a time you solved it.

## §3 Research the company

- Check the About page and recent blog or LinkedIn posts, and mention something specific (e.g. a mentorship program launch, connected to your own mentoring).
- Avoid generic praise ("Your company is a leader in the industry"). Better: a specific initiative tied to your background.
- Fallback without a hook: focus on the role ("the team's focus on X resonates with my experience in Y").

## §4 Template (paraphrased structure)

1. Greeting to the hiring manager by name.
2. Who you are (title, two key skills), the role, and how your background solves a problem they mentioned.
3. One or two achievements, with a specific story and numbers or outcomes.
4. What excites you about the company (a specific value, project, or initiative), an optional personal connection, and the impact you want to make.
5. Thanks, contact details, sign-off.

- "The more you customize it the better." "You want to make it sound human."

## Common mistakes

- Repeating the resume: give context, not a list of jobs.
- Forgetting the "why should they care": always link a skill to their need (e.g. a workflow-streamlining requirement answered with a tool that cut meeting time 20%).
- **Underqualified**: be honest and focus on growth, naming the skill, how long you've built it, through what, and one specific achievement. A career changer cited a year of volunteer hiring coordination with 100+ applicants screened.

## Final tip

- "Read it aloud." If it sounds stiff or robotic, rewrite it as if explaining your fit to a friend.
- TL;DR: connect your experience to their problems. "Clarity beats cleverness every time."
